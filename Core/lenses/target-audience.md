# Target Audience

> Who is this for? (And who is it NOT for?)

*Designing without an audience in mind produces work that lands for no one in particular.*

---

## Core idea

Every artifact (a doc, a workflow, a system, a piece of writing) is for someone. Sometimes the audience is obvious. Often it's fuzzy or default-generic, which usually means default-no-one. When the audience is unclear, the work drifts: too technical for newcomers, too basic for experts, too long for skimmers, too compressed for first-timers.

Target Audience is the lens that asks: *who am I serving here?* Naming the audience tightens the rest of the design. And the inverse (*who is this NOT for?*) is just as important. Trying to serve everyone is the fastest path to serving no one.

---

## How it shows up

- **Specific over generic** — "the new student arriving on Day 1" beats "users"
- **Inclusion AND exclusion** — naming who it's NOT for is design discipline, not arrogance
- **Audience shapes voice** — same content lands differently for different ears
- **Audience shapes form** — a quick reference and a deep guide need different shapes
- **The audience may not be human** — sometimes the receiver is the AI, or future-you, or both

---

## When it fires

- About to write something and feel directionless
- The work feels good but isn't landing
- Trying to make one thing serve too many people
- Tempted to add "just in case" sections for hypothetical readers
- After other lenses, as a sanity check on who the result actually serves

---

## The test

If you can't name a specific person (or specific type of person) who will read or use this, the work is being designed for an imaginary average. Find the actual person.

If the answer is "everyone," that usually means no one in particular. The fix isn't to design for fewer people, it's to know who's at the center even when others will read.
