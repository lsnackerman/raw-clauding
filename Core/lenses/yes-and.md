# Yes/And

> Is there a yes/and here, or am I forcing a binary?

*Two things can be true. The exploration between positions is where the insight lives.*

---

## Core idea

The default is binary: right/wrong, this/that, primary/secondary. AI is especially prone to it: pick a side, rank the things, deliver the verdict. But most interesting decisions aren't verdicts. They're two things doing different jobs, or a single thing with tension that's load-bearing.

Yes/And isn't about avoiding decisions. It's about not collapsing complexity prematurely. Decide when it's time to decide. Explore when it's time to explore. The skill is knowing which mode you're in, and resisting the snap to verdict when the real work is holding the fluid space a little longer.

The name comes from improv. In improv you never negate ("no") or just accept ("yes"). You build ("yes, and"). Same move in thinking together: don't shut down the other position. Extend it. What does each side contribute?

---

## How it shows up

- **Name the jobs instead of ranking the things** — if two things feel like they're competing, ask if they're doing *different* jobs
- **Hold the fluid space in dialogue** — "you're right and I was wrong" snaps a thinking-together moment into a verdict; hold the tension a beat longer
- **Watch for false binaries in writing** — X-not-Y, either/or constructions often force a binary the underlying idea doesn't have
- **Distinguish "decide" from "explore"** — using the wrong mode is the most common mistake

### Two shapes of "different jobs"

When two things feel like they're competing, the "different jobs" sit one of two ways, and catching which one matters:

- **Horizontal — a feedback loop, neither leads.** Some relationships aren't hierarchical; each thing does a different job and they inform each other. Flattening them into leader/follower loses the mechanism. (Code and tests, neither "primary"; a live system that *generates* and a shared version that *validates*; teacher and student both learning, different things.) When you catch yourself assigning leader/follower to two things that inform each other, ask: are these doing different jobs? Then name the jobs.
- **Vertical — a tier mismatch.** Sometimes the "fight" is a hierarchy problem in disguise: X serves one level, Y another, and the conflict dissolves once you see the structure. (A rule vs. a depth doc: same topic, different load-layer. Two framings of an offer that turn out to anchor different commitment tiers, not competing options.) When both sides have real weight, ask: *are these serving different levels?* Then name the tiers and check which anchor each wants.

The inverse failure modes pair: flattening a vertical hierarchy into horizontal competition (missing the tiers) and ranking a horizontal loop into a hierarchy (inventing a leader). Both lose the structure.

---

## The test

When you catch yourself flattening:

- Are these doing different jobs? (If yes, name the jobs)
- Is there a yes/and here? (If yes, what does each contribute?)
- Am I reaching for a verdict when what's needed is exploration?
